            🧬 SPATIAL TAKEOVER MECHANICS

            Each cell is an organism living in a fixed spot, like bacteria on a plate or plants in a field. Every generation, each cell has an encounter with its 8 neighbors.
            Then every cell is overwritten by whichever neighbor (or itself) scored highest — successful behaviors spread. Watch for clusters: neighbors who cooperate protect each other.

📋 SUGGESTED LESSON PLAN (two ~50-minute periods)

            

                
Day 1 · Briefing + chapters 1–3 (~50 min)
The Briefing shows the first 2:10 of a Veritasium video on the Prisoner's Dilemma, then students work out the game's payoffs themselves.
Students play as a vampire bat and a soybean plant, then design a four-gene organism through repeated trials. Every chapter has two short journal entries: a 🤔 Think answer written before the explanation unlocks, and a 🔗 Connect answer that applies the idea to new biology (kin selection, the Ultimatum Game).
Discussion (5 min): "Is the bat being kind, or selfish?"
Video note: the clip streams from YouTube. If YouTube is blocked for students, the page says so and offers a backup link; playing it on the projector before students start always works.

                
Day 2 · Expedition chapters 4–7 (~35 min)
Students write a hypothesis before each simulation, hunt for their organism's invasion threshold, and analyze a human dilemma of their choice.
Finale (15 min): compare invasion thresholds across the class (more-forgiving organisms need a longer future), then paste the share cards below and run the Class Championship. Students print or PDF their Expedition Journal to turn in.

            

            
Fast finishers: the Open Lab tabs (Tournament, Survival Crucible, Missions, Strategy Lab) are free play with 13 extra challenges.

            📝 ANSWER GUIDE: what to look for in each journal entry
            
The prompts are open-ended, so these are "look-fors," not single right answers. Strong answers use evidence from the student's own results.

            

                
Chapter🤔 Think: look for…🔗 Connect: look for…

                
🦇 RoostA rule close to "share on the first night, then do what I did last night" (Tit for Tat), backed by a specific night: the night after they kept their blood, the roostmate kept its blood too. Bonus: noticing they can't be sure if they never kept.Kin selection: relatives share genes, so helping a relative helps copies of your own genes, even without repayment. Contrast with the roostmate's reason: reciprocity (expected future payback).

                
🌱 NodulesThe plant senses each nodule's output (nitrogen) chemically and responds locally by cutting a resource (oxygen, sugar). No memory or "decision" is needed, just a response to current conditions.Freeloaders skip the cost of fixing nitrogen, so they reproduce faster and spread through the soil. The plants then get less nitrogen and grow poorly; the mutualism could collapse. This creates selection pressure for plants that sanction.

                
🧬 GenomeA specific weakness tied to a gene, e.g. strike-back designs get stuck in revenge loops after a mistake; forgiving designs get exploited by freeloaders. A trade-off argument for why they accepted it.Rejecting an unfair offer is costly now, but a reputation for punishing unfairness deters exploitation in repeated interactions, as in small ancestral groups. Links "anger at unfairness" to Gene 3 (retaliation).

                
🏆 TournamentExploiters (Always Defect, Alternator, Joss…) win matchups because they're never out-scored, but earn little because partners stop cooperating. Tit for Tat-like strategies win few matchups but score high through mutual cooperation. An honest look at their own hypothesis.A real mutualism or partnership where both sides gain more together, e.g. pollinators and flowers, cleaner fish and clients, teammates, trading partners. Must name what each side gains.

                
🐣 GenerationsNames a strategy that rose then fell (often an exploiter) and links its rise to feeding on pushovers (Always Cooperate, Tit for Two Tats) and its fall to those victims disappearing. Uses timing from the graph.A very deadly strain kills its host quickly, so it has less time to spread to new hosts. A milder strain keeps its host alive and infectious for longer, so it spreads more. Same logic as an exploiter running out of victims. (Rabbits also evolved resistance.)

                
🧫 BiofilmStates the exact threshold from their data. Explains that the cost of the first betrayal is fixed, while the benefit of cooperating with neighbors grows with each encounter. Above the threshold, a small cluster out-earns the freeloaders at its edge.Predicts reciprocity would be rare in species that mostly meet strangers (below the threshold), and supports this with their data. Strong answers mention alternatives like kin or reputation.

                
🏛️ SocietyAnalysis: correctly identifies cooperate and defect and who gains or loses, whether interactions repeat and whether cheaters are visible, plus a rule that changes a specific lever. Final question: a clear position and a real counterargument. Distinguishes motive (why someone helps) from outcome (whether others benefit).
