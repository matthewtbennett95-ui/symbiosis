Strategy Lab

Design your own strategy with snap-together rules or code, test it, then save it to use everywhere.

How the Strategy Lab works (tap to read) Your strategy is a set of instructions that runs every round of an encounter. It can see everything that has happened so far between you and your partner, and must answer with C (cooperate) or D (defect). Use the Rule Builder to snap rules together, or switch to Code to write JavaScript. Every famous strategy can be remixed.

Tournament
Recreate Axelrod's 1980 contest: every strategy plays every other one, and the highest average score wins.

        
How the tournament works (tap to read)
            Every entrant meets every other entrant. Points from every encounter are added up. This is exactly how Axelrod ran his 1980 contest (200 rounds per encounter, and each program also played a copy of itself).
            Pay attention to the Wins column — the champion often wins surprisingly few head-to-head matches.

Simulation
Survival Crucible
Release several species into one dish. Points become energy, so watch which ones spread and which ones starve.

        
How free-roaming works (tap to read)
            Organisms drift around the dish. When two bump into each other, they have an encounter. Cooperating together gains energy, defecting together loses energy, and exploiting a cooperator feeds the exploiter at the victim's expense. Living costs a little energy too.
            Organisms with enough energy split in two; organisms that run out starve (shown with a skull). Bigger glow = more energy.
        

        
How the grid works (tap to read)
            Each cell is an organism living in a fixed spot, like bacteria on a plate or plants in a field. Every generation, each cell has an encounter with its 8 neighbors.
            Then every cell is overwritten by whichever neighbor (or itself) scored highest — successful behaviors spread. Watch for clusters: neighbors who cooperate protect each other.

Simulation
Evolution Engine
Start with random strategies and let natural selection, not a designer, decide what survives.

        
How the Evolution Engine works (tap to read)
            In 1987, Robert Axelrod stopped designing strategies by hand and let evolution do it. He used a genetic algorithm, a method from artificial intelligence that copies natural selection:
            
    
                
  1. Start with a population of random strategies. Each one's genes are its chances of cooperating after each kind of round.
    2. 
                
  2. Every strategy plays. Its average score is its fitness.
    3. 
                
  3. The fittest reproduce. Offspring copy their parent's genes with small random mutations, and sometimes mix two parents' genes (crossover, like sexual reproduction).
    4. 
                
  4. Repeat for many generations.
    5. 
            

            Nobody tells the population what a good strategy looks like. Watch what it discovers.

Game
The Commons
Fish a shared lake with five neighbors. Can the village keep catching fish without emptying the lake?

        
How the Commons works (tap to read)
            Until now, every dilemma had two players. Many real ones have many: a shared fishery, a forest, clean air, or the sugar that yeast cells release for their neighbors.
            You are one of six villagers fishing the same lake. The fish regrow fastest when the lake is about half full, and not at all when it is empty.
            Whatever you catch is yours, but the lake belongs to everyone.


            In 1968, ecologist Garrett Hardin argued that shared resources are doomed: each person gains by taking more, so the "tragedy of the commons" is inevitable.
            Political scientist Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize, 2009) found real communities that avoided the tragedy for centuries. Try her rules and see if they work.

Cleaning Station
You are a cleaner fish. Earn as much food as you can without losing your reputation.

        
How the Cleaning Station works (tap to read)
            You are a cleaner wrasse, a small reef fish that runs a "cleaning station." Bigger fish visit to have parasites picked off their skin. Eating parasites is an honest meal.
            But cleaners would rather bite off a mouthful of the client's protective mucus, which is more nutritious and hurts the client.


            Clients choose which cleaner to visit, and other fish nearby are often watching. This time you will rarely meet the same client twice, so what keeps you honest is your reputation.

Game
Family Ties
You are a ground squirrel. When a predator appears, do you risk your life to warn the others?

        
How Family Ties works (tap to read)
            You are a Belding's ground squirrel in a mountain meadow. When a predator appears, you can give an alarm call. Calling warns everyone nearby, but it draws the predator's attention to you.
            Some of your neighbors are relatives. Relatives share some of your genes: a sister shares about half, a cousin about an eighth, a stranger none (beyond what everyone in the population shares).
            Your score counts every copy of your genes that survives: in you, and in your relatives.

Game
Face a Classmate
Two players, one Chromebook. Choose in secret each round, and nobody knows when the match will end.

        
How it works (tap to read)
            This is the Prisoner's Dilemma with real people. Each round you both choose to cooperate or defect without seeing the other's choice, using the same points as the rest of the lab. Because the match can end after any round from round 6 on, there is no safe "last round" to cheat on.

Teacher

Suggested lesson plan (two ~50-minute periods)

            

                
Day 1 · Briefing + chapters 1–3 (~50 min)
The Briefing shows a 2½-minute edited clip of Veritasium's Prisoner's Dilemma video (three sections, played back to back), then students work out the game's payoffs themselves.
Students play as a vampire bat and a soybean plant, then design a organism from genes (brain type, testing, reactions, memory) through repeated trials. Every chapter has two short journal entries: a Think answer written before the explanation unlocks, and a Connect answer that applies the idea to new biology (kin selection, the Ultimatum Game).
Discussion (5 min): "Is the bat being kind, or selfish?"
Video note: the clip streams from YouTube. If YouTube is blocked for students, the page says so and offers a backup link; playing it on the projector before students start always works.

                
Day 2 · Expedition chapters 4–7 (~35 min)
Students write a hypothesis before each simulation, hunt for their organism's invasion threshold, and analyze a human dilemma of their choice.
Finale (15 min): compare invasion thresholds across the class (more-forgiving organisms need a longer future), then paste the share cards below and run the Class Championship. Students print or PDF their Expedition Journal to turn in.

            

            
Exit ticket: the Check-in Quiz in the Open Lab (10 questions, printable score). Fast finishers: the Open Lab (Tournament, Survival Crucible, Missions, Strategy Lab, Head-to-Head, Evolution Engine) is free play with 15 extra challenges. Extension ideas: after Chapter 7, have students play The Commons twice (no rules, then monitoring + sanctions) and explain the difference. In the Evolution Engine, have students compare an evolved champion with the organism they designed by hand in Chapter 3, then enter both in a tournament. Which design process worked better, and why?

            ANSWER GUIDE: what to look for in each journal entry
            
The prompts are open-ended, so these are "look-fors," not single right answers. Strong answers use evidence from the student's own results.

            

                
ChapterThink: look for…Connect: look for…

                
RoostA rule close to "share on the first night, then do what I did last night" (Tit for Tat), backed by a specific night: the night after they kept their blood, the roostmate kept its blood too. Bonus: noticing they can't be sure if they never kept.Kin selection: relatives share genes, so helping a relative helps copies of your own genes, even without repayment. Contrast with the roostmate's reason: reciprocity (expected future payback).

                
NodulesThe plant senses each nodule's output (nitrogen) chemically and responds locally by cutting a resource (oxygen, sugar). No memory or "decision" is needed, just a response to current conditions.Freeloaders skip the cost of fixing nitrogen, so they reproduce faster and spread through the soil. The plants then get less nitrogen and grow poorly; the mutualism could collapse. This creates selection pressure for plants that sanction.

                
GenomeA specific weakness tied to a gene, e.g. strike-back designs get stuck in revenge loops after a mistake; forgiving designs get exploited by freeloaders. A trade-off argument for why they accepted it.Rejecting an unfair offer is costly now, but a reputation for punishing unfairness deters exploitation in repeated interactions, as in small ancestral groups. Links "anger at unfairness" to the "After they defect" gene (retaliation).

                
TournamentExploiters (Always Defect, Alternator, Joss…) win matchups because they're never out-scored, but earn little because partners stop cooperating. Tit for Tat-like strategies win few matchups but score high through mutual cooperation. An honest look at their own hypothesis.A real mutualism or partnership where both sides gain more together, e.g. pollinators and flowers, cleaner fish and clients, teammates, trading partners. Must name what each side gains.

                
GenerationsNames a strategy that rose then fell (often an exploiter) and links its rise to feeding on pushovers (Always Cooperate, Tit for Two Tats) and its fall to those victims disappearing. Uses timing from the graph.A very deadly strain kills its host quickly, so it has less time to spread to new hosts. A milder strain keeps its host alive and infectious for longer, so it spreads more. Same logic as an exploiter running out of victims. (Rabbits also evolved resistance.)

                
BiofilmStates the exact threshold from their data. Explains that the cost of the first betrayal is fixed, while the benefit of cooperating with neighbors grows with each encounter. Above the threshold, a small cluster out-earns the freeloaders at its edge.Predicts reciprocity would be rare in species that mostly meet strangers (below the threshold), and supports this with their data. Strong answers mention alternatives like kin or reputation.

                
SocietyAnalysis: correctly identifies cooperate and defect and who gains or loses, whether interactions repeat and whether cheaters are visible, plus a rule that changes a specific lever. Final question: a clear position and a real counterargument. Distinguishes motive (why someone helps) from outcome (whether others benefit).
